More on them later. So first, what do we really mean when we say AI is a black box, and how did this idea became so widely accepted? The idea goes back to the earliest days of modern AI, when Geoffrey Hinton and his colleagues were exploring a different way to represent knowledge inside neural networks. There were two competing ideas at the time: local representations and distributed representations- And Geoffrey Hinton was a strong advocate for the second one, Imagine.

We wanted a network to recognize these concepts- cars, cats and dogs. The localist representation argued that each of these concepts had to have their own dedicated units: One unit for cars, one for cats and another for dogs. Note that this isn't a strictly feedforward network. It is really simple.

Activation of the cat unit means the network is thinking of cats. But the distributed representation was a completely different view. Hinton argued that each concept doesn't have to have a dedicated unit.

The concept emerges from the activation patterns and each neuron could be activated for several concepts. For example, a dog could be the activation of units 1, 2, and 3, and deactivation of 4 and 5.. But none of the neurons mean anything on their own. The concept emerges from the pattern. It's like a symphony: No single musician is the song. The song only exists in the coordinated pattern of the play.

What happened was that the distributed representation ended up winning over the field, Although that's not completely true, and we'll come back to it. But this approach had multiple attractive features that helped it grow in popularity. First, automation: The network learns useful patterns directly from data, without us having to understand what each individual unit does. Efficiency: N units can encode far more than N concepts. Graceful degradation:

Losing a few units blurs representation rather than wiping it out. Automatic generalization: Similar concepts: share units. So what you learn about one partly carries over to similar ones. Content-addressable memory:

A partial pattern can help you bring back the whole one Sort of like how the brain works. Distributed representation became a massive trend and the localist view was sometimes mocked as the grandmother cell, pointing the unlikely possibility that there is a single neuron in your head that represents your grandmother. But distributed representation also had a pretty obvious flaw: If the individual neurons have no meaning, then as the network grows in size and as the connections become more dense and complicated, it becomes harder and harder to understand what the network is thinking. And that's exactly what happened.

That was the first thing that obscured our understanding of the inner workings of AI, But nobody was losing sleep over that back in the 1980s. So decades passed and in 2022, Anthropic went looking for a ray of hope, but instead found another disappointing feature of neural networks inside models of superposition. In the decades between 1980s and 2020s, which we didn't get into, researchers realized that not only every unit in the network is meaningless alone, but also you zoom in on a neuron and it fires for a fox, but it also fires for a car and a bullet train. Essentially, each neuron can, and often is participating in multiple very unrelated patterns, And they called this issue superposition or polysemanticity. Each neuron is a musician in multiple unrelated songs.

So in the toy models of superposition, Anthropic was trying to show why superposition might not be a flaw, but they ended up proving the point even further. They showed when you have more concepts than dimensions, the model mathematically does not have the option to store them all as orthogonal concepts. It either has to identify the most statistically important features and throw the rest away, or store them in non-orthogonal, overlapping directions, meaning the concepts are bleeding into each other's space.

Anthropic argued that superposition might be a feature because by tolerating some noise, a smaller network can approximate a much bigger one, And that keeps the network a lot more nuanced than the alternative of throwing away the least statistically relevant concepts. But in the process, Anthropic also mathematically proved that any layer of a network can pack in more concepts than it has neurons. They provided evidence that superposition is not only happening, but it is practically unavoidable. So the black box theory was considered untouchable at this point. The neurons don't mean anything alone. They have overlapping, unrelated responsibilities, and there is no way to avoid it.

If that's true, then there is no hope of interpreting the activation patterns, especially as networks keep getting bigger, unless someone provides a completely new perspective that challenges some of the assumptions that we held since 1980s. Nick Cammarata co-authored the early work on mechanistic interpretability at OpenAI and, in his own words, came away pessimistic because it was just as messy as everyone described. But now, with the modern agentic tools like Claude and GPT, you can get much more done in less time. So he revisited some of the earlier research And the result is this beautiful work that gives us reason to be hopeful again. Neural networks are already clean. He wrote:

"The canonical evidence of polysemanticity and superposition that convinced much of the field, including me, that networks pack concepts arbitrarily was misread. What looked like arbitrary packing was mostly a motive I'll call recursive generalization". So what Nick did was he went back and re-examined some of the most famous examples of superposition with the help of agentic AI models. For example, we went back to the famous neuron that was firing for a fox, a car and a bullet train. And it is still was the case.

But while further examining the neuron, they realized the images that triggered the highest activations contained some sort of pointy shape, a specific V-shaped geometry that exists in the fox image, the hood of a car and the nose of a bullet train. Then they confirmed it by looking at something called a negative pole. Neurons don't always activate positively. They also repel concepts by negative activation, And the negative pole for V-shape is the O-shape in this case. So they tried a dog with a pointy snout and a dog with a O-shaped one, And the activation was really clear. The neuron was excited about the pointy snout and actively repelled the O-shapes, Although they were both dogs.

They went back and combed through so many previous experiments and realized that some of these neurons might have odd or counterintuitive jobs, but they could almost always find a common thread between the seemingly unrelated patterns. But didn't Anthropic prove that the network has limited number of directions and it has to eventually cram unrelated things into a single neuron? Turns out the AI training process is way a smarter than we initially gave it credit for. They showed the networks do not abandon their fundamental organizing principles when they are squeezed for space. A tiny, restricted model does not panic and pack a boat and a dog in the same bucket.

It instead drops resolution and creates broader categories. For example, if you look at the final layer of a tiny, intentionally squeezed model, you won't find a tangled mess. Instead you find a single, highly logical neuron that groups all ground vehicles on its positive side and all the boats and planes on the negative side. It doesn't become a jumble of unrelated topics. It zooms out, loses resolution.

But the final question is: imagine the network has already used up all of its space and more information just keeps pouring in. How does it keep everything organized when it never planned for it in advance? To demonstrate this, Nick followed a single neuron to see how it evolves throughout training. This is the most beautiful experiment in the whole paper.

It shows that the model is using a mechanism he calls recursive generalization. Instead of packing unrelated things into the same box, a neuron starts by grouping highly similar, simple things together. Then it branches out conceptually. For example, you have a single neuron that starts out doing a very basic job. It fires when it sees the letters IY next to each other, like the name Miyazaki.

At this point it's just part of a tiny spell checker looking for a specific letter combination. But then, as the model reads more text and learns that neuron generalizes and it starts firing for the letters DIY. It still contains IY, but DIY is much more meaningful as the acronym for do-it-yourself.

But then it takes one more recursive conceptual leap. The neuron actually starts firing strongly for the full English phrase do-it-yourself, which doesn't even contain IY or DIY as a combination of letters. The neuron generalized from a literal spelling fragment to an acronym containing that fragment, to the abstract underlying meaning of the acronym itself. What's stunning about this discovery is that it suggests that the localist view was closer to reality than we gave it credit for. Nick writes:

"With better tools and modern models, the situation looks quite different. In preliminary audits of Gopher 3- 32 billion parameters, we estimate that roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job, often with related offshoots, and another 7% have two related jobs, and only 2 to 4% are not yet understood. So if most neurons have well-defined job. why do we have so many weak activations? How do we account for the neurons that aren't exactly sure they are supposed to fire or not? Nick's team went in and manually removed 75% of the weak activations and the accuracy fell only 1%.

They then tried the opposite and removed the strongest 7% activations and the accuracy fell from 72 to 4%, showing that most weak activations aren't really contributing to the output. The funny thing is, if neurons have well-defined jobs, then the localist view wasn't that far away from the distributed representation. It's funny because they ended up being basically the same theory. You might actually have a grandmother cell in your head.

If you want to detect dogs, cats and cars, you can use a distributed network that has five units, like the example we had at the beginning. But if each unit ends up having a well-defined job through training like detecting furry- four legs, barks, wheels, metal- then this is a local network. In fact, if the theory holds, any network could be seen as both. A representation is local with respect to some set of entities. If each one of those entities gets its own dedicated unit, like furry, four legs, etc. It's distributed with respect to other entities If each one is a pattern over shared units like dogs, cats and cars. So what looked like a tangled mess may actually be like a organically grown city: Complicated but not random. It's actually full of order And if this work proves correct by peers, there is now hope that we can develop very reliable tools to monitor AI models. Once we understand how the city works, we can guide its evolution, cutting wasted energy, fixing broken pathways and promoting what makes it safer.
